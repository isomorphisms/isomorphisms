# Eisenbud, econometrics, and learning geometry

## 2014

In 2014 I picked up **David Eisenbud's** *Commutative Algebra with a View Toward Algebraic Geometry*. I immediately realized that I needed to rethink how I understood econometrics.

That was not the same as suddenly understanding algebraic geometry. I have worked toward it a little at a time.

Ordinary econometrics taught me much about estimators, residuals, regressors, and sampling uncertainty. Eisenbud suggested another set of questions. What mathematical object is the parameter space? Which equations define it? Where does the space split into components? Which combinations of parameters cannot be identified? What does the local tangent space look like? When does its dimension change?

These questions do not replace statistics. They describe structure that conventional data workflows can easily hide.

The book is [Springer GTM 150, first published in 1995](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-1-4612-5350-1). The year 2014 is when I encountered it, not its publication date.

## Not another decorative layer of mathematics

I do not want to put algebraic vocabulary on regression output merely to make it look sophisticated.

I want the algebra to earn its place.

A model may have polynomial restrictions, latent variables, constraints, or symmetric representations. These determine which parameter values are possible. Some structures can be tested exactly, simplified by elimination, decomposed into components, or drawn.

If none of that changes the calculation or the interpretation, the extra mathematics has not helped yet.

This is one reason to keep an executable reference next to theoretical notes. The mathematical claim should eventually have a small example, an independent check, and a case that shows its limits.

## Start with familiar interactions

Consider a model with a measured response $y$ and two explanatory variables $x$ and $z$:

$$
y=\beta_0+\beta_xx+\beta_zz+\beta_{xz}xz+e.
$$

The interaction term $xz$ is still an ordinary regressor once its data column is constructed. But its place in the polynomial ring $\mathbb R[x,z]$ invites questions about algebraic relations among regressors and the design's parameterization.

Suppose all observed rows satisfy $x=z$. Then the design columns for $\beta_x$ and $\beta_z$ coincide. The two coefficients cannot be separately identified from those rows without additional information.

The problem is not only a large standard error. It is a structural rank defect.

For larger interaction families—$x$, $z$, $w$, $xz$, $xw$, $zw$, $xzw$, powers and mixtures—the set of possible data configurations and constraints can become complicated. Polynomial relations and their syzygies can expose redundancy that matrix notation alone may conceal.

But a regression design matrix is not automatically an algebraic variety of scientific interest. It must be tied to a substantive model, a sampling scheme, or a testable restriction.

## Tangent spaces connect the pictures

An algebraic set may be defined by equations

$$
F_1(p)=\cdots=F_m(p)=0,
\qquad p\in\mathbb R^n.
$$

At a point $p$, the Jacobian matrix $DF(p)$ records first-order changes in those equations. The kernel of that matrix is the **Zariski tangent space** when working in the corresponding algebraic setting over the chosen field.

The tangent-space dimension can reveal a rank drop. At singular points the tangent space can have more directions than a smooth nearby piece would suggest.

A small concrete case is the rank-one condition on a $2\times2$ matrix:

$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix},
\qquad ad-bc=0.
$$

Its gradient, ordered by $(a,b,c,d)$, is

$$
(d,-c,-b,a).
$$

At a nonzero rank-one matrix the gradient is nonzero. At the zero matrix all four components vanish. The determinantal variety is singular there.

Rank constraints like these also arise in statistical models, including factor analysis. So a geometric singularity can be relevant to identifiability and the behavior of inference.

There is a direct visual project here: render a low-dimensional slice, show its tangent approximation, then move toward a point where the Jacobian rank changes. The picture and the symbolic derivative should agree.

## Ideals describe possible worlds

An ideal generated by model equations represents more than one chosen numerical solution. It records all polynomial consequences of those equations.

An elimination step can remove latent coordinates and give conditions on observables. A primary decomposition can expose components. A syzygy can expose a relation among generators. Localization can focus attention near a chosen part of the space.

Each operation has mathematical hypotheses and computational costs. A package that reports a Gröbner basis or primary decomposition should preserve the coefficient ring, term order, input ideal, and algorithm assumptions.

The result should not be mistaken for a proof about a physical system whose actual mechanics were only approximately encoded.

The question is: can a computer-algebra procedure tell us something that a numerical optimizer could not easily reveal?

That is worth testing one example at a time.

## Curvature is not a small inconvenience

A linear statistical approximation can be locally useful but globally misleading.

A smooth nonlinear map $f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$ has a Jacobian. A parameter manifold may curve through the ambient parameter space. A rank change can make a seemingly harmless inverse highly unstable.

The full second-order behavior contains Hessians and mixed terms. Two uncertainties can interact through those terms even if their sources are statistically independent.

This is not a reason to abandon regression. It is a reason not to confuse the local linear approximation with the entire model.

The [uncertainty and vehicle Jacobian note](uncertainty-and-vehicle-jacobians.md) follows this question using physical rotations and measurements. A rigid body is mathematically clear; an actual car also has compliant parts, difficult reference frames, and correlated measurement errors. That mixture makes the geometry consequential.

## Statistics already has an algebraic literature

I did not invent the connection.

**Mathias Drton, Bernd Sturmfels, and Seth Sullivant** wrote *Lectures on Algebraic Statistics*. **Drton and Sullivant** also described polynomial and semialgebraic statistical models in *Algebraic statistical models*.

There is established work on graphical models, factor analysis, contingency tables, toric models, Markov bases, determinantal ideals, and singular statistical models.

The interesting question for this project is which established results are worth translating into working code and pictures. Some may improve a test. Others may only produce an expensive change of notation. I want to find out rather than assume either answer.

## One direction through the code

~~~text
econometric model
  → constraints and statistical assumptions
  → polynomial/rational structure where justified
  → ideal, variety, components, tangent space
  → symbolic and numeric checks
  → rendered examples and interactive controls
  → residuals and actual observation
  → revised model
~~~

Some parts can be represented in [Eisenbud–Harris notes](https://github.com/isomorphisms/Eisenbud-Harris), [Fulton work](https://github.com/walnut-burgundy/fulton), and [Econometrician](https://github.com/bl4ckb4ll/econometrician). [Algebraic Variety Explorer](https://github.com/isomorphismes/algebraic-variety-explorer-mobile) and [SURFER](https://github.com/isomorphismes/algebraic-variety-explorer-mobile) provide a path toward rotatable real zero sets.

A picture is evidence of a particular computed representation, not automatically proof that the chosen ideal is the right scientific model. Preserve equations, hypotheses, component identities, and source references with each render.

## Learning without a university program

A doctoral program is not a workable route for my life. That does not make geometry inaccessible.

I can read a chapter. Translate a theorem into a small computation. Draw the example. Compare the result with an independent package. Preserve a failure. Follow the bibliography. Return to the same question later with better tools.

I do not expect every experiment to end in an executable theorem. Some are useful because they change which questions I can ask.

The mainline is not a credential. It is a growing body of exact examples, geometric intuition, and verified connections.

## A few precise experiments

**Polynomial regression rank:** build a design from $1,x,z,xz$; force $x=z$; show the nonidentifiable coefficient direction. Compare symbolic rank with an SVD under explicit scaling.

**A determinantal surface:** study $ad-bc=0$; compute its Jacobian; show regular points and the singular origin. View three-dimensional slices and keep the omitted dimension explicit.

**A constrained hinge:** introduce sine and cosine coordinates, add $s^2+c^2=1$, and eliminate one latent coordinate. Compare the algebraic projection to direct trigonometric evaluation.

**Local versus inverse uncertainty:** put several distinct error sources through the same nonlinear map. Track their first- and second-order interactions. Invert only on a justified local branch, or return a feasible region.

**Quantized Jacobian:** preserve one fixture and compare Float32, Float16, and the declared lower-precision formats. Report observed − reference, not only a green/red outcome.

The experiments connect books, code, and a rotatable image. That is enough reason to do them.
