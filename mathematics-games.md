# Mathematics games

A surprisingly large amount of mathematics can be made into something you can touch.

Move a root and watch the coefficients change.

Move a coefficient and watch the roots reorganize.

Drag zeros and poles through a complex phase portrait.

Rotate an algebraic surface.

Reflect a point repeatedly and watch a Coxeter group act.

Change one parameter and watch a singularity appear.

Twenty years ago, many of these ideas might have become a Java applet, a Mathematica notebook, or a classroom demonstration.

Now they can be videos, tiny interactive programs, or standalone phone apps.

That changes what is worth building.

## One idea can be the whole app

A mathematical program does not have to be a general-purpose environment.

It can exist to show one theorem, one construction, one corollary, one picture, or one surprising relationship.

That smallness is a feature.

A person who opens GeoGebra still has to know what they want to construct.

A narrowly focused mathematical app can instead say:

**Here is the thing. Touch it.**

The interaction itself can carry some of the explanation.

Move this point.

Rotate this object.

Change this coefficient.

Cross this boundary.

Now see what stayed invariant and what changed.

This is closer to a mathematical toy than a general mathematical workstation.

## Current examples

[Wegert](https://github.com/isomorphismes/wegert) makes complex rational functions visible through phase portraits. Zeros and poles can be placed and dragged directly in the picture.

[Coefficient Root Dance](https://github.com/isomorphismes/coefficient-root-dance) lets the user move between the coefficients and roots of a polynomial by touch. Drag one side and the other side responds.

[Algebraic Variety Explorer](https://github.com/isomorphismes/algebraic-variety-explorer-mobile) puts real algebraic surfaces on an Android phone: rotate them, zoom them, and change the defining polynomial.

[Coxeter](https://github.com/isomorphismes/coxeter) is building around reflections, root systems, Dynkin diagrams, and the algebraic structure behind repeated geometric transformations.

The short videos are useful too. Not every mathematical idea needs interaction. Sometimes a moving picture is enough to expose something that is difficult to see in static notation.

## Hand holding is not the enemy

General-purpose mathematical software is powerful, but power does not tell a person what is interesting.

A narrowly designed program can do more of that work.

It can choose the initial example.

It can constrain the controls to mathematically meaningful moves.

It can make the invariant visible.

It can prevent irrelevant operations.

It can arrange the picture so that the interesting event is hard to miss.

It can even be built around one page of Euclid.

That is not dumbing mathematics down.

It is designing the encounter.

## Mathematics can be playable without becoming trivial

"Game" here does not have to mean points, winning, or a cartoon reward loop.

The play can be mathematical.

There is pleasure in prediction:

What happens if I move this root through that one?

There is pleasure in control:

Can I make the surface develop this shape?

There is pleasure in surprise:

Why did the picture change discontinuously there?

There is pleasure in recognition:

That complicated motion was only a reflection composed with another reflection.

A good mathematical toy gives the user enough structure to discover those moments without requiring a textbook chapter before anything moves.

## Cheap software changes the scale

The important economic change is that the audience for one mathematical idea does not have to justify a large software project.

If a useful interactive can be generated, checked, packaged, and distributed cheaply, then extremely specific mathematics becomes viable.

A program for one corollary.

A visualization for one proof.

A touch experiment for one map.

A moving diagram for one family of polynomials.

A small app for one piece of Euclid.

I am building the mathematics I personally find interesting. Other people can build different ones.

The larger possibility is that mathematics no longer has to wait for somebody to build the universal educational platform before it becomes interactive.

We can just make the little thing.
