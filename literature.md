# literature on your phone

Phones are very good at filling empty minutes.

A looped video, a joke, a stunt, a game, or some other small piece of entertainment can be exactly what somebody wants. There is nothing wrong with that.

But the same device can also make room for pleasures that take longer to unfold.

Some of the greatest literature in the world is about things that are not particular to an era or a platform: fear, love, shame, ambition, grief, loyalty, betrayal, exile, death, hope, memory, God, politics, friendship, and the strange ways people justify themselves to themselves.

The problem is not that these works have disappeared.

The problem is that they often arrive surrounded by friction.

The book may be difficult. The language may be old. The translation may be opaque. A reader may need a note, another translation, the original language, a map, a name, or five minutes of conversation before the passage opens up.

A phone can remove much of that friction.

## Mi Dante

[Mi Dante](https://github.com/isomorphisms/mi-Dante) begins with the *Inferno*.

The working corpus keeps several layers separate:

- Dante's Italian;
- the historical Spanish translation by Manuel Aranda y Sanjuán;
- a separate working translation produced during reading;
- bilingual views for comparison;
- reading notes;
- and later reading editions built from those materials.

The point is not to pretend that translation is mechanical.

A useful reading can ask:

- What did this translator preserve?
- What did he omit?
- What did he make explicit?
- What moved?
- What became clearer?
- What became weaker?
- Which word matters here?
- Would another translation expose something the first one hides?

That turns the phone into something closer to a reading companion than an electronic page.

## A different kind of immediate gratification

Deep reading is often described as the opposite of modern phone use.

It does not have to be.

The interface can still be immediate.

Open the app. Read ten lines. Tap a difficult phrase. Compare the Italian. See what another translator did. Ask a question. Continue.

There does not need to be a semester, a syllabus, a lecture hall, or an obligation to finish a canonical book because somebody said an educated person is supposed to finish it.

The reader can simply encounter something good.

Some days that may be a canto.

Some days it may be four lines.

The important thing is that the deeper option exists at the same distance from the thumb as everything else.

## Cheap enough to be specific

This is another place where cheap software changes what is worth making.

A commercial publisher might reasonably ask whether there is a large market for a new Dante reading environment.

That is not the interesting question here.

If the software is cheap enough to build and distribute, it can be made because one particular way of reading one particular book seems worthwhile.

The same pattern can extend to other works.

Not "build the universal literature platform."

Build a good way to read Dante.

Then perhaps a good way to read another difficult work.

The software can be as particular as the work itself.

## Keep the work in front

The technology should not become the attraction.

The aim is not to turn Dante into points, streaks, badges, or a stream of generated summaries.

The aim is to make the text easier to enter without flattening it.

A good reading tool should help when help is needed and disappear when it is not.

The reward is still the poem.
